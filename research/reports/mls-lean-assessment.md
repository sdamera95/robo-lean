# MLS in Lean: source assessment and research opportunities

**Baseline date:** 2026-10-08  
**Primary source:** Murray, Li, and Sastry, *A Mathematical Introduction to Robotic Manipulation* (1994), [local PDF](../../references/MLS.pdf). Page references below use the book's printed numbering; the corresponding PDF page is approximately the printed page plus 18.

## Scope and evidential status

This assessment is a starting point for translating most of MLS's mathematical robotics content into Lean while leaving the choice of robotics, control, or learning application open. The source review covered the book's organization and selected definitions and derivations in Chapters 2–8 and Appendices A–B. It was **not** a line-by-line audit of 474 PDF pages. Chapters 1 and 9 supply context and research outlook, rather than a separate theorem inventory in this assessment. Page references identify the source passages for the claims below; the proposed formalization and applications are research designs, not results already proved in Lean.

The repository uses Lean and mathlib v4.34.1. The initial setup compiled the real-number identity `RooboLean.add_sq` using `ring` in [the smoke module](../../RoboLean/Smoke.lean); its axiom report listed `propext`, `Classical.choice`, and `Quot.sound`. This evidence concerns that algebra theorem only; no robotics theorem has been demonstrated. The effort required for a broad MLS translation is uncertain until a small end-to-end pilot exposes the missing mathematical interfaces and proof obligations.

*2026-10-09 UTC relocation note:* the preceding paragraph records the original setup baseline. ## What the book offers as formalization targets

MLS has a useful dependency structure: rigid motions and twists support kinematics; kinematics and inertia support dynamics; both feed grasping, constrained mechanisms, stability, and nonholonomic planning. The following targets are more precise than an ambition to encode every equation or paragraph.

| Source | Mathematical content with clear proof obligations | Formalization pressure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ch. 2, pp. 23–63 | Rotation groups, Rodrigues' formula, rigid motions, twists, adjoint transformations, and wrench–twist pairing. | Define frame-aware maps and keep the book's twist coordinate order \((v,\omega)\). Matrix formulas must match the chosen multiplication and frame conventions. |
| Ch. 3, pp. 83–141 | Product-of-exponentials forward kinematics, spatial/body Jacobians, inverse-kinematic subproblems, workspace notions, and closed-loop constraints. | Separate configurations, joint limits, frames, branches, and regularity assumptions. Closed-loop mobility needs independent constraints; actuator and kinematic singularities are distinct. |
| Ch. 4, pp. 171–193 | Manipulator inertia and Coriolis structure, energy identities, computed-torque control, and PD stability claims. | State positivity and exact-model hypotheses explicitly; compare each controller theorem with the full dynamics. |
| Ch. 5, pp. 213–251 | Contact models, grasp maps, force closure, hand-object manipulability, and rolling constraints. | Parameterize the contact law and friction cone; represent rank, positivity, smooth-chart, and curvature assumptions. |
| Ch. 6, pp. 277–310 | Constrained robot dynamics, tendon actuation, and hierarchical composition of mechanisms and controllers. | Track constraint rank and feasible forces; state when an interconnection preserves a control property. |
| Chs. 7–8, pp. 326–381 | Frobenius/Chow controllability, chained forms, sinusoidal steering, and approximate steering. | Distinguish existence from a constructive control law, and exact from approximate steering. |

The group law of \(SE(3)\) is \((p_1,R_1)(p_2,R_2)=(p_1+R_1p_2,R_1R_2)\); the adjoint in the book's \((v,\omega)\) order has blocks \(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}R&\widehat p R\\0&R\end{smallmatrix}\right]\) (Ch. 2, pp. 35–59). These are candidate first-pilot identities because a frame or coordinate-order error here propagates through Jacobians, wrenches, and dynamics. Wrenches are dual to twists through the power pairing (pp. 61–63). Appendix A warns that \(SE(3)\) has no bi-invariant positive-definite Riemannian metric and that naive Jacobian singular values mix rotational and translational scales and depend on frame choice (pp. 427–430). A formal library should therefore expose the chosen metrics and units rather than silently treating a six-vector's Euclidean norm as physically canonical.

For an open chain, the product-of-exponentials formula uses joint axes expressed in one reference frame (Ch. 3, pp. 85–87). The book's ideal revolute/prismatic configuration space is a product of circles and lines; joint limits define a further admissible subset (pp. 83–87). The spatial and body Jacobians arise by differentiating forward kinematics (pp. 115–117). The familiar \(\tau=J^\mathsf{T}F\) identity is a virtual-work statement that requires the wrench and Jacobian to use compatible frames, under the stated frictionless idealization (pp. 121–122). Inverse kinematics includes special Paden–Kahan subproblems and branch/degeneracy issues (pp. 97–108); those constructions should not be advertised as a generic inverse-kinematics solver. MLS also distinguishes reachable from dextrous workspace by their quantifiers over poses and orientations (p. 95).

In dynamics, MLS uses a symmetric positive-definite mass matrix and a Christoffel-form Coriolis matrix satisfying the skew identity for \(\dot M-2C\) (Ch. 4, Lemma 4.2, p. 171). A library proof must justify positivity at the chosen model level. For an assembled expression \(M=\sum_i J_i^\mathsf{T}I_iJ_i\), positive-semidefinite spatial inertias alone yield only a positive-semidefinite \(M\); positive-definite \(I_i\) and injectivity of the stacked Jacobian are sufficient for positive definiteness. This is a mathematical inference from the quadratic form, not a claim that MLS lists precisely those hypotheses. A moving reference trajectory also requires a suitable positive energy or error Lyapunov function: a potential-only expression does not control nonzero velocity. The computed-torque result (Proposition 4.8, pp. 190–192) assumes exact cancellation of the modeled terms and appropriate positive gains.

The contact results have sharp domains. MLS introduces ideal rigid-contact models with specified contact behavior (Ch. 5, pp. 213–214). Its force-closure criterion uses a surjective grasp map and an interior strictly positive internal force under an unbounded contact-force model (Proposition 5.2, pp. 223–224). Hand-object manipulability has the range condition \(\operatorname{range}(G^\mathsf{T})\subseteq\operatorname{range}(J_h)\) (pp. 237–238). Rolling-contact formulas require smooth local surface charts and an invertible relative-curvature map (pp. 248–251). They are unsuitable as unconditional global identities. Chapter 6 similarly depends on constraint rank, manipulability, and feasible contact cones (Lemma 6.1, pp. 277–281). The tendon relation \(\tau=Pf\) assumes ideal massless, lossless, tension-only transmission (pp. 294–299). Its hierarchical operations explicitly compose mechanisms and control components (pp. 303–310), but stability of the resulting interconnection requires a separate theorem. The inverse-Jacobian operation on p. 305 assumes the relevant Jacobian is invertible.

The nonholonomic chapters are attractive but easy to overstate. The Frobenius and Chow results concern smooth drift-free systems and local controllability through distribution/rank hypotheses (Ch. 7, pp. 326–331). A rank theorem establishes reachability under its assumptions; it does not by itself synthesize controls. For constructive reversal arguments, the admissible control set must include signed controls near zero. Chapter 8 proves exact steering for a sinusoidally driven chained form under its stated assumptions (Proposition 8.3, pp. 363–366), while its more general steering construction is approximate (pp. 380–381). Neither is a general obstacle-avoidance algorithm. The preface explicitly leaves sensing and general collision avoidance outside the book's scope (pp. xiv–xv).

Appendix B's Mathematica packages, including Screws.m and RobotLinks.m, expose computational operations such as TwistExp, forward kinematics, and SpatialJacobian (pp. 435–438). These are useful examples for checking conventions and constructing small regression cases. Their code has limited argument checks and is not a certified numerical implementation.

## One source passage to treat cautiously

There is an apparent missing assumption or compensation in the plain PD discussion following the computed-torque result. Proposition 4.9 (p. 193) applies \(\tau=-K_v\dot e-K_pe\) to the full dynamics given in equation 4.47 (p. 190), whose gravity term \(N(q,\dot q)\) is defined earlier (p. 171). The displayed closed-loop equation 4.52 omits that term. If \(N(q_d,0)\ne0\), the desired fixed configuration is not an equilibrium under that bare PD input. This is a direct consistency check of the cited formulas, **not** a general errata judgment or a Lean counterexample already implemented. Formalization should state a gravity-free setting, add appropriate gravity compensation, or prove a claim about a shifted equilibrium as dictated by the intended model.

## A Lean architecture that keeps applications open

Three related deliverables would give the translation both mathematical breadth and operational value:

1. **A reusable mathematics layer.** Define rotations, poses, twists, wrenches, adjoints, exponentials, and compatible Jacobians. Prove frame-change, derivative, power-pairing, and energy identities before embedding a specific robot. The book's coordinate formulas can be theorems over these definitions, with explicit equivalence to matrix expressions.
2. **A modeling layer.** Describe joints, rigid bodies, frames, inertia, actuation, limits, contacts, and constraints with stated idealizations. Derive open-chain kinematics and dynamics compositionally. Separate physical assumptions, such as rigid bodies and exact parameters, from algebraic consequences of those assumptions. Treat a formal model as a specification of an ideal system, not evidence that any calibrated physical robot obeys it.
3. **A certificate interface.** Let external symbolic, numerical, or learned tools propose parameters, trajectories, bounds, or controllers; check in Lean the finite certificate or analytic theorem that makes a stated property follow. Numerical algorithms need an explicit refinement or error-bound argument before their floating-point output inherits a real-number theorem. Lean is a proof checker and mathematical programming environment, not an automatic solver for arbitrary inverse kinematics, optimization, or contact planning.

This is a proposed division of labor, not a commitment to a particular Lean API. Present mathlib documentation includes [orthogonal/unitary group machinery](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/UnitaryGroup.html), [classical Lie algebra structures](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Algebra/Lie/Classical.html), [matrix exponentials](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Analysis/Normed/Algebra/MatrixExponential.html), [manifold derivatives](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Geometry/Manifold/MFDeriv/Defs.html), [ODE existence and uniqueness](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Analysis/ODE/ExistUnique.html), and [Gronwall estimates](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Analysis/ODE/Gronwall.html). These show relevant mathematical building blocks, not ready-made robotics proofs or compatibility with this project's pinned Lean 4.34.1 environment. The current matrix-exponential documentation has a TODO concerning the determinant/trace relation, so its presence does not establish every identity needed for \(SO(3)\). Lean's [proof-validation reference](https://lean-lang.org/doc/reference/latest/ValidatingProofs/) explains the trusted-kernel boundary; claims about computer-assisted results should identify axioms, external certificates, and approximation assumptions where they matter.

## Research directions enabled by a broad translation

**Robot design and workspace coverage.** A design statement can quantify over a task region \(W\) and admissible configurations \(Q_d\): \(\forall x\in W,\ \exists q\in Q_d,\ FK_d(q)=x\). That proves position reachability only for the specified ideal kinematics. Orientation requirements, collision avoidance, a continuous path, and dynamic feasibility need additional statements. For a planar two-revolute arm with positive link lengths \(a,b\) and unrestricted joints, the position workspace is the annulus \(|a-b|\le\|x\|\le a+b\). This gives a tractable first design certificate: check that a prescribed radial band lies within those bounds. Equal lengths allow the base-centered disk of radius \(a+b\), but the origin is a singular configuration, so reachability should not be confused with dexterity. External optimizers can search designs; Lean can check the final quantified certificate or conditions that imply it.

**Control and planning.** The same model can support checked computed-torque or Lyapunov conditions, constraint preservation, and special-case nonholonomic steering. Useful proofs must retain the exact-model, gain, admissibility, and invariant-region assumptions. A trajectory theorem should state what motion is achieved, for which initial states and disturbances, and whether it is local, global, exact, or approximate. Contacts and mode changes add hybrid-system obligations beyond the smooth chapters of MLS.

**Learning with guarantees.** A learned residual dynamics model, candidate Lyapunov function, barrier function, or policy can enter as an untrusted proposal. A theorem can use a proved bound on residual error over a region, plus an invariant-region argument, to certify a stability or safety statement. Pointwise sampled fit, training loss, or an apparently plausible neural network is insufficient evidence for a regionwide bound. The formal target should state the region, norm, disturbance set, and margin. How to obtain tractable certificates for expressive models remains an open research choice.

**Reusable semantics for broad robotics work.** Typed frames, units, coordinate conventions, model composition, and proof-carrying numerical interfaces could make the library useful beyond a single manipulator. The most valuable applications are not yet determined: robot design, controller verification, grasp analysis, planning, and learning each exercise different parts of MLS. A pilot across kinematics and one dynamics/control theorem can measure actual library friction before choosing an emphasis.

## Decisions and limits for the next phase

The first formal milestone should prove a small connected chain, for example the \(SE(3)\) group law and adjoint action, a one- or two-joint product-of-exponentials forward-kinematics result, and the corresponding Jacobian/power identity. Such a pilot tests conventions and mathlib fit more directly than isolated theorem counts. A second pilot can test the dynamics assumptions by deriving positive definiteness for a concrete arm and formalizing one energy or controller statement. Comparing each formal statement with the book's exact hypotheses is essential, especially where the prose suppresses model assumptions.

Major open choices include a matrix-first versus abstract Lie-group core, the representation of coordinate frames and physical units, whether analytic ODE proofs or certified algebraic certificates provide the earliest research value, how much contact and hybrid behavior to include, and what numerical refinement burden is acceptable. No estimate for translating “most of MLS” is reliable yet. The source review supports a plausible dependency map and concrete first obligations; only a completed pilot can establish the engineering cost and determine where theorem statements need to diverge from the printed presentation.
